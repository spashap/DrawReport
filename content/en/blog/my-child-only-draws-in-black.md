“For weeks now she has only used black. Is something wrong?” It is one of the most common
worries parents bring us, and the short answer is almost always no.

Here is what a run of black drawings usually means, what it cannot tell you, and what is
actually worth looking at on the page.

## Why children reach for one color

Most of the reasons are wonderfully boring:

- **Black makes the best line.** It is the highest contrast you can get on white paper, and
  the result looks crisp and deliberate. Children notice that.
- **It is the marker that still works.** Anyone who has dug through a marker box knows that
  black tends to outlive the rest.
- **One choice is easier than twelve.** Fewer decisions means the drawing starts sooner.
- **Outlines are the job right now.** There is a normal stretch of development where the
  shape of a thing matters far more to a child than filling it in.
- **They like how it looks.** Comics, cartoons and games lean heavily on black outlines, and
  children copy what they admire.

## What the color does not tell you

The idea that black means sadness, or that dark equals anxiety, is folklore. It shows up in
a lot of parenting articles because it is a satisfying story, but the evidence for reading a
child’s feelings from their color choices is thin, and acting on it tends to worry parents
about the wrong thing.

We never read a child’s mood from the colors they pick, and we would gently suggest you
don’t either.

## What is typical at each age

| Age | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| 2–4 | Scribbles and big simple shapes. Single-color stretches are extremely common. |
| 4–6 | Stick figures and symbols. Black often outlines a favorite character. |
| 6–9 | Scenes and stories arrive. Look for variety across a week, not on every page. |
| 9–12 | Real style preferences form. Black-and-white can be a deliberate aesthetic. |

## What to look at instead

If you want to get something out of the drawing, look past the color at what your child’s
hand and eye actually did:

- **Line quality.** Are the lines steady? Confident, or hesitant and gone over twice?
- **Whether shapes hold together.** Do the parts of a figure connect where they should?
- **Use of the page.** Crowded into a corner, spread across the sheet, or balanced?
- **Detail.** Small repeated marks take real control to make.
- **Story.** Is something happening, or is it a single object sitting on the page?

Those are visible, they are real, and they develop in fairly predictable stages. That is the
part of a drawing that will actually tell you something.

## How to ask about it

The trick is to be curious rather than corrective, and to ask about the picture rather than
about the color:

- “Tell me what’s happening here.”
- “Which part did you like drawing most?”
- “If you added one more color, which one would you grab?”

What you want to avoid is handing your child an interpretation to agree with. “Are you feeling
sad?  Is that why it’s all black?” teaches them that the drawing was a test they got wrong.
Their words will take you somewhere more interesting than your guess would have.

If the single-color stretch really is nagging at you, the gentlest nudge is an invitation
rather than a correction: “Want to add who lives in this house?” Leave a few other colors
within reach and let it go at that.

## When it is worth talking to someone

A drawing is not the place to look for an answer to a serious worry, and neither is an
article. If your child seems withdrawn, if sleep or appetite has changed, if school has
become hard, or if something just feels off to you, the color of their markers is a footnote.
Talk to your pediatrician, or to a teacher who sees your child every day. They can ask
questions no picture can answer.

Most of the time, though, the other colors come back on their own, and nobody ever finds out
why they left.
