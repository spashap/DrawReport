If your child keeps reaching for black, navy, deep purple or charcoal, the drawings can start
to feel heavy. It is worth knowing that color is one of the weakest signals a drawing carries,
and one of the most over-read.

## Why children pick dark colors

**Contrast.** Dark markers make crisp lines on white paper. Anything meant to look like an
outline, a comic panel or an inked drawing needs them.

**Fewer decisions.** A small set of markers means the drawing starts sooner. Some children
find a full box genuinely overwhelming.

**Style.** Graphic novels, anime and games are full of night scenes and heavy black linework.
Children who admire that look copy it, and by around age nine a lot of them are copying it on
purpose.

**The subject asked for it.** A thunderstorm, a cave, outer space and a black cat all need
dark colors. This one gets missed constantly.

## There is no color code

You will find charts online that assign a feeling to each color. They make for a satisfying
read and they do not hold up. A child who draws in gray is not telling you something a child
who draws in yellow is hiding.

What the color does tell you is more modest and more useful: which markers were within reach,
what your child was drawing, and what look they were going for. Here is that version of the
chart.

| Color | Why it usually turns up |
|---|---|
| Black | Outlines, comic and “inked” styles, high contrast, silhouettes |
| Navy and dark blue | Night skies, water, uniforms, storms |
| Dark purple | Fantasy, magic, space, anything meant to look otherworldly |
| Charcoal and gray | Shading practice, pencil comfort, buildings, rain, robots |
| Dark green and brown | Forests, animals, dinosaurs, camouflage, autumn |

## What is typical at each age

| Age | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| 2–4 | Big scribbles, single-color stretches. Dark markers show up best, which is half the appeal. |
| 4–6 | Black outlines with a little color inside. Cartoon characters drive a lot of the choices. |
| 6–9 | Scenes and stories. Expect variety across a week rather than on every page. |
| 9–12 | Deliberate style. Comic, minimalist and moody looks are all normal here. |

## What to look at instead of color

- **Line pressure.** Light and floaty, or heavy enough to emboss the next sheet?
- **Control.** Steady lines, or rushed and smudged with a lot of erasing?
- **Figures and proportion.** How big is your child in their own drawings, relative to everyone else?
- **Use of the page.** Balanced, crowded into one corner, or the same empty area every time?
- **Whether the subject changes.** A week of different scenes reads very differently from the same scene seven times.

None of these is a verdict either. They are simply the parts of a drawing that are actually
visible, which makes them the parts worth your attention.

## Two examples

**The comic artist.** Nine years old, black and gray, clean outlines, panels ruled with the
edge of a book. Relaxed while drawing, happy to explain the plot, and the plot changes every
week: robots, then a soccer match, then the dog. This is a child who has found a style they
like. There is nothing here to solve.

**The same storm, every day.** Seven years old, the same night storm for a month, pressed hard
enough to dent the paper, a very small figure in the corner, a lot of erasing. Nothing here
diagnoses anything either. But the sameness, the pressure and the erasing are all worth
mentioning to someone who knows your child, especially if school or home has shifted lately.

## How to ask about it

Ask about the drawing, not about the colors:

- “Tell me about this part.”
- “How did you get it to look like that?”
- “What happens next?”

Steer clear of handing over an interpretation for your child to agree with. “Dark colors mean
you’re sad, right?” closes the conversation and teaches them that drawing is a test.

## When to talk to someone

If your child seems withdrawn, if sleep or appetite has changed, if school has become hard, or
if something simply feels off to you, that is worth a conversation with your pediatrician or
with a teacher who sees your child daily. Bring the drawings if you like. Just don’t let the
colors be the reason, and don’t let them be the reassurance either.
