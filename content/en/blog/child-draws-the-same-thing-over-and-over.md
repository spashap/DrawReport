faq:
  - q: My child has drawn the same thing for months. Is that too long?
    a: Months is ordinary, especially for a subject a child loves. Look at the first and the most recent side by side. If the drawings are getting more detailed, more controlled or more varied in small ways, the repetition is doing its job. If they are identical and the child seems bored or anxious while drawing, it is worth a gentle new invitation.
  - q: They only copy the same cartoon character. Does that count as creative?
    a: Yes. Copying is how nearly every artist learned, and matching a character’s shapes takes real observation and control. Children who copy a lot usually start changing things, giving the character new clothes or a new scene, when they are ready.
  - q: Should I buy a how-to-draw book?
    a: If your child wants one, yes. Step-by-step books give a child new formulas to repeat, and repeating formulas is exactly the stage they are in. Leave it lying around rather than presenting it as a fix for the repetition.
  - q: I read that repetitive drawing can be a sign of autism.
    a: Intense, repetitive interests are one of many things clinicians consider, and they never consider them from drawings alone. Plenty of children who love one subject with their whole heart are not autistic, and drawing a subject repeatedly is not a screening sign. If you have wider questions about how your child communicates, plays with others or copes with change, talk to your pediatrician about those. The drawings are not the evidence either way.
  - q: My child only draws Minecraft. What do I do?
    a: Ask them to explain it to you. Block-based worlds are full of structure, planning and rules, and a child who draws them is usually building something in their head. Interest in the subject will pass or broaden on its own. The skills built while drawing it stay.

The pile on the kitchen table is forty drawings of horses. Or trucks, or the same princess in
the same dress, or a house with the same smoking chimney and the same sun in the corner. The
question that comes with the pile is whether a child who draws the same thing every day is
stuck.

For nearly every child, the answer is no, and the repetition is the point.

## Repetition is how children learn to draw

Somewhere between four and seven, children stop making new marks each time and start building
formulas. A house is a square with a triangle on it. A person is a circle, a rectangle, four
lines. Once the formula works, the child repeats it until it is reliable, and only then starts
to vary it. Researchers call the formula a schema, and the repeating stage lasts for years.

It is the same way children learn a song, by singing it forty times, or learn to catch, by
throwing the ball at the wall until the hands stop thinking. Each repetition looks identical
to an adult. It rarely is. Put the first horse next to the fortieth and the legs are in the
right place, the mane has strands, and the line no longer wobbles at the neck.

## Why the same subject

- **Interest.** Horses, dinosaurs, trucks, a game, a show. Children draw what they think about,
  and at six they think about one thing at a time with great intensity.
- **Competence feels good.** A child who can draw a good cat will draw cats. Drawing something
  new means being bad at it again, and children are not fools.
- **It got a reaction.** One horse was praised, or a friend asked for one, and the horse became
  a product line.
- **Friends draw it.** At school, drawing subjects spread through a class like a rumor.
- **Comfort.** In a busy or unsettled week, a drawing you already know how to make is a small
  place where everything goes to plan.

## What is typical at each age

| Age | What usually repeats |
|---|---|
| 3–4 | The same circle-person, over and over, with different names. |
| 4–6 | The same house, sun and flower. The same figure standing for everyone. |
| 6–9 | The same character or animal, refined each time. Series, comics, a “style.” |
| 9–12 | The same subject pursued to mastery: fan art, one animal, one kind of car. |

## What the repetition is teaching

The interesting part of forty horses is the difference between horse number three and horse
number thirty. Line control, proportion, the number of details, whether the ground appeared,
whether the horse acquired a rider and then a field and then a story. Development shows in the
sequence, and a child who repeats gives you the sequence for free.

Keep a few, dated. Look at them a couple of months apart. That is where the developmental
information in a drawing actually lives.

## The question parents don’t ask out loud

Some parents searching this topic are wondering about autism, and it is better to say so than
to leave it hanging.

Intense, repetitive interests are one of a number of things a clinician looks at when thinking
about autism, and they look at them in the child, in how the child communicates, plays with
other children, handles change and uses language. They do not look at them in drawings, and a
child drawing the same subject repeatedly is not a screening sign. Most children with a
consuming interest in one subject are simply children with a consuming interest.

If you have wider questions about your child’s development, the right move is to raise them
with your pediatrician directly. Describe what you see across the day. The drawings can be
mentioned, and they will not be the evidence in either direction.

## Should you steer them somewhere else

Mostly not, and never with “draw something else.” A child who is told their subject is boring
hears that their competence is boring, and the usual result is less drawing rather than
different drawing.

Invitations work where instructions do not:

- **Extend the subject.** “What is the horse doing today?” “Where does it live?” “Who rides
  it?” A horse with a field is a landscape. A horse with a rider is a person drawing.
- **Change the materials.** The same cat in chalk on the sidewalk, in paint, on a huge sheet of
  brown paper, is a new problem for the hand.
- **Draw alongside them.** Draw your own bad horse. Children love a parent who draws worse than
  they do, and they will start giving you advice.
- **Give the subject a job.** A birthday card for Grandma with a horse on it. A sign for the
  bedroom door. A comic with three panels.

## How to ask about it

- “What’s different about this one?”
- “Which one is your best? What makes it the best?”
- “What does this one still need?”
- “Can you teach me how you do the legs?”

The last one is worth trying even if you can draw. Explaining a formula out loud is how
children start to notice it, and noticing it is the first step to changing it.

## When it is worth a conversation

The repetition itself is not the thing to watch. What is worth noticing is a change in how the
child is while drawing. Tears when a drawing is not perfect, tearing up pages, drawing that
looks driven rather than absorbed, or a child who has stopped enjoying the subject and cannot
seem to stop drawing it. Those are about how the child is doing, and if they come alongside
changes in sleep, appetite or mood, they belong in a conversation with your pediatrician, with
the drawings as one of several things you noticed.

For the rest, the pile of horses is exactly what it looks like: a child getting very, very good
at horses, in public, one page at a time.
