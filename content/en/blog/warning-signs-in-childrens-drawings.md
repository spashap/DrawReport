faq:
  - q: Is there any reliable list of warning signs in children’s drawings?
    a: Not in the sense the internet means. No single feature of a drawing, on its own, has been shown to reliably identify a troubled child. What professionals pay attention to is change over time in a child they know, combined with how the child is doing everywhere else.
  - q: My child drew a person with no hands. I read that means guilt or helplessness.
    a: That idea comes from projective tests written in the 1940s and it did not survive later research. Hands are among the hardest things to draw, and children leave them off for years. Look at whether your child draws hands on other days before reading anything into one page.
  - q: Do psychologists actually use these lists?
    a: Some clinicians use drawings as a way to start a conversation with a child, and a few use scored versions as one small input among many. Sign-by-sign interpretation of the “black means depression” kind is not standard practice, and the research reviews have been unkind to it.
  - q: What about paying for a professional drawing analysis?
    a: Be wary of anyone who offers to tell you what is wrong with your child from a photo of a drawing. A drawing can show you where a child is developmentally and give you things to ask them about. It cannot diagnose, and a service that says otherwise is overselling.
  - q: Should I keep a drawing that worried me?
    a: Yes, with the date on the back. If your worry turns out to be nothing, you have a drawing. If it turns out to be something, you have a record, and the person you talk to will be glad of it.

You noticed something in a drawing, you searched a phrase, and a list came back. Black means
depression. Missing hands mean guilt. A house with no door means a withdrawn child. Big teeth
mean aggression, heavy pressure means anxiety, a figure at the edge of the page means low
self-esteem.

The lists are confident, they travel well, and they are mostly wrong. Here is where they come
from, what each item more plausibly means, and what is genuinely worth your attention instead.

## Where the lists come from

Most red-flag lists are descendants of the projective drawing tests developed between the
1940s and the 1960s: Draw-a-Person, House-Tree-Person, the Kinetic Family Drawing. The idea was
that a child projects their inner life onto the page, so a clinician could read the page like a
letter. Each test came with a catalog of “indicators” and what each one supposedly revealed.

When researchers went back and checked whether those indicators actually distinguished troubled
children from typical ones, most did not. The same features turned up in the drawings of
perfectly content children at about the same rate. Later reviews of the evidence were blunt
about it. The lists survived anyway, because they are satisfying to read, and satisfying
stories are hard to kill.

## The usual items, and what they more likely mean

| What the list says | What it is supposed to mean | What it more often is |
|---|---|---|
| Lots of black | Sadness, depression | Black makes the strongest line. It is the marker that still works. |
| No hands or fingers | Guilt, helplessness | Hands are hard. Most children skip them for years. |
| Very large head | Anxiety, self-focus | Heads come first and get the most room. Normal until eight or so. |
| Tiny figure in a corner | Low self-worth | Ran out of confidence in the pencil, or ran out of page. |
| House with no door or windows | Withdrawal | A five-year-old’s house formula. Doors arrive later. |
| Heavy pressure, dark lines | Tension, aggression | A strong grip and a soft crayon. Also, enthusiasm. |
| Lots of erasing | Insecurity | A child who has started to care how it looks. Usually a good sign. |
| Big teeth, claws | Aggression | Monsters are fun. Teeth are what make a monster a monster. |
| Rain, clouds, no sun | Sadness | It was raining. Or the sun was already drawn on the last page. |
| Figure floating, no ground | Instability | Nothing stands on a baseline until around six. |

None of these are impossible as signals. All of them are far more likely to be about drawing
skill, age, materials, and what the child watched last night.

## Why single signs fail

A drawing feature means different things at different ages. A missing torso is universal at
three and unusual at nine. Reading either without knowing the age is guessing.

Children copy. A style borrowed from a cartoon, a friend or an older sibling carries the
“signs” of that style, not of the child.

Drawings vary from day to day for reasons that have nothing to do with mood. Tiredness, the
pen that was available, how much time there was, who was watching.

And most importantly, the features on these lists are common. If nine in ten children draw
people without hands at some point, a missing hand cannot be information about the tenth. That
is the whole reason the research went badly for the lists.

## What is genuinely worth your attention

What professionals watch for is not a sign on a page. It is change, in a child they know,
across time, alongside everything else. Put in parent terms:

- **A child who drew freely and has stopped**, or whose drawings have become anxious, torn up
  or hidden, when they used to be shown off.
- **Distress during drawing**, rather than absorption. A child who is upset while drawing is
  telling you something about now, not about the picture.
- **The same worrying content coming back for weeks**, when it used to vary, *and* something
  else has shifted too: sleep, appetite, school, friendships, mood at home.
- **A child who tells you a drawing is about something real that happened to them.** The words
  matter far more than the picture.

Notice that every one of these is about the child and the pattern, and none is about a single
feature of a single page. A month of gloomy drawings from a child who is otherwise thriving is
a month of gloomy drawings.

## Two situations that are not judgment calls

**Drawings that show your child harming themselves.** Treat this as urgent. Contact your
pediatrician or a mental health professional now, keep the drawing, and do not spend the evening
interpreting it.

**Sexual content that shows knowledge beyond what a child of that age would ordinarily have,
or a child saying a drawing shows something someone did to them.** Stay calm, do not
interrogate, and do not lead. Say that you are glad they showed you and that they are not in
trouble. Then contact your pediatrician or your local child protection helpline the same day.
They will know how to have the next conversation in a way that does not put words in a child’s
mouth. Keep the drawing, dated.

Both of these are rare. Both are also the reason it is worth looking at your child’s drawings
at all, so it would be wrong to leave them off a page about what is and is not a warning sign.

## What to do with a drawing that worries you

Ask about it before you decide what it means. Children will narrate a drawing happily if you
ask what is happening rather than why they drew it, and the narration is usually mundane.

Then look at the last month of drawings rather than this one. Then look at the child:
sleeping, eating, playing, talking about their day. If all of that is fine, you have a drawing.
If it is not, the drawing is a footnote, and the conversation to have is with your pediatrician
or a teacher who sees your child every day.

## How to ask

- “Tell me about this one.”
- “What’s happening here?”
- “Who is this, and what are they doing?”
- “What happens next?”

Avoid handing your child an interpretation to agree with. “Is this because you’re sad?” teaches
a child that drawings are tests and that there was a wrong answer. Their own words will take
you somewhere more useful than any list.
