You’ve probably seen articles claiming a drawing reveals a child’s hidden fears, or that
a particular color “means” something is wrong. It’s a tempting idea. It’s also not how
children’s drawings actually work.

## What a drawing is not

A single drawing is **not** a psychological or medical assessment. The color black doesn’t
mean sadness. One dark picture doesn’t reveal a hidden problem. Child psychologists are
careful here for a good reason: reading a child’s feelings, or a diagnosis, from a picture
isn’t supported by solid evidence, and it leads parents to worry about the wrong things.

## What a drawing actually shows

What a drawing *does* show, clearly and usefully, is **skills**. How a child uses the page.
How steady the lines are. Whether shapes hold together. How your child picks and puts down
color. Whether there’s a story. All of it is visible and real, and it develops in fairly
predictable stages as children grow (scribbling, pre-schematic, schematic, and then dawning
realism).

## The honest way to read one

Our approach is simple: tie every observation to something **you can actually see**
in the drawing, and read it against what’s typical for the child’s age. A confident outline,
a thoughtful layout, neat coloring-in: these point to developing drawing skills rather than to a
diagnosis. From there, the useful question is “what’s already going well, and what could we
gently try at home?”

That’s the whole idea: a calm look at what your child can do, not a verdict on
who they are.
