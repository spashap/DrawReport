faq:
  - q: Is it normal for a 2-year-old to draw faces?
    a: It happens, and it is on the early side. A circle with two dots at two is a lovely moment, and it is just as typical for the first face to arrive closer to three and a half. Both children are on the same road. Neither is ahead in any way that will still be visible at seven.
  - q: My 5-year-old still draws people as a head with legs. Should I worry?
    a: Usually not. The tadpole figure most often gives way to a body somewhere between four and five, and a year either side of that is common. If your child is otherwise using their hands well, holding a pencil comfortably and adding new details now and then, the body will come.
  - q: My child seems to have skipped scribbling and went straight to shapes. Is that possible?
    a: More often they scribbled somewhere you did not see, or the scribbling stage was short. Children with a lot of exposure to older siblings or to drawing at daycare sometimes move through the early stages quickly. It is not a problem either way.
  - q: Do girls draw ahead of boys?
    a: On average girls tend to draw people with more detail a little earlier, and the difference is small compared with the range inside each group. Interest and practice explain far more than sex does. A boy who loves drawing trucks will out-draw most of his class in trucks.
  - q: How can I help my child move to the next stage?
    a: Mostly by not trying to. Keep paper and something to draw with where they can reach it, sit and draw alongside them without correcting, and ask about the pictures. Stages arrive on their own schedule, and children who are pushed to draw “properly” often draw less.

“My two-year-old already draws faces.” “My five-year-old still draws stick people.” Parents
compare, and the comparing tends to produce worry in one household and pride in another, when
in fact both children are doing exactly what children do.

Children’s drawing develops in a sequence that is remarkably consistent across cultures and
generations. The sequence is fixed. The ages attached to it are not. Here is what each stage
looks like, roughly when it tends to appear, and how to think about a child who is earlier or
later than the chart.

## Why there are stages at all

A young child does not draw what they see. They draw what they know. A four-year-old knows a
person has a head, eyes, a mouth and legs, so that is what goes on the page, and the fact that
real people also have necks and torsos is not yet part of the picture in either sense.

Drawing also depends on the hand. Closing a circle, stopping a line where you meant to, making
two marks the same size, these are motor skills that mature over years. A drawing is a record
of what the child understands and what the child’s hand can currently do, layered on top of
each other. That is why the stages are so predictable, and why they are so much more
interesting than the folklore about hidden meanings.

## Around 1 to 2½: marks and scribbles

The first drawings are not drawings of anything. A toddler discovers that moving the crayon
leaves a trail, and the trail itself is the fun. The whole arm swings from the shoulder, the
marks go off the edge of the paper, and the same joy shows up on walls and steamed-up windows.

Scribbles gradually become more controlled: back-and-forth zigzags, then circular loops, then
dots and dashes made on purpose. Nothing here is meant to represent anything, and asking “what
is it?” usually gets a puzzled look.

## Around 2½ to 3½: named scribbles and the first circle

Two things happen close together. The child starts closing a circle, which takes real
coordination, and they start naming their marks. The naming usually comes after the drawing,
not before: a scribble is finished, looked at, and announced to be Mommy or the dog. Tomorrow
the same scribble may be a car.

This is the first time the child connects a mark with an idea, and it matters far more than
how the mark looks.

## Around 3 to 4: the tadpole person

Almost every child on earth draws the same first person: a circle for the head, two lines
coming straight out of it for legs, and often two more for arms. Eyes appear early because
faces are what children look at most. A mouth follows, then hair, then fingers sprouting from
the ends of the arm lines.

The tadpole figure is not a mistake and not a sign of anything missing. It is a stage with its
own name in the research, and it sorts itself out on its own. The body arrives when the child
decides the person needs somewhere to put buttons.

## Around 4 to 6: symbols, floating things and the sun in the corner

Figures get a torso, usually a second circle or a rectangle under the head. Arms move from the
head to the body. People acquire clothes, fingers in bunches, and the occasional neck.

Everything floats. Houses, people, trees and the family car sit wherever the hand landed,
because the child is drawing a collection of things they know rather than a scene they saw.
Size follows importance more than reality, which is why a child may be taller than the house.
Colors are chosen for pleasure, so purple grass is normal. And the sun, with rays, goes in the
top corner of nearly every page in the country.

## Around 6 to 9: baselines, x-ray pictures and stories

A line appears along the bottom of the page for everything to stand on, sometimes with a strip
of blue sky along the top and nothing in between. Children work out a formula for each thing
they draw, a house, a person, a tree, and reuse it, tweaking it a little each time.

This stage produces some of the most charming drawings a child will ever make. You get
“x-ray” pictures where you can see the people inside the house, fold-over pictures where the
people on both sides of a street lie flat as if the page were opened out, and scenes with a
clear story: who is doing what, where they are going. Color turns realistic. Details multiply.

## Around 9 to 12: realism arrives, and so does self-criticism

Children start trying to draw things as they look. Figures overlap instead of standing in a
row. Proportions improve, the baseline disappears into a horizon, and there are first attempts
at depth and perspective. Clothes become specific, hairstyles matter, and there is a strong
pull toward copying styles the child admires, from comics to game art.

The cost of realism is that children can now see the gap between what they wanted and what
they got. “I can’t draw” tends to be said for the first time in this stage, and a lot of
children stop drawing for pleasure around eleven or twelve because of it. The ones who keep
going are usually the ones nobody corrected.

## The stages in one table

| Rough age | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| 1–2½ | Scribbles for their own sake. Whole-arm movements, marks off the page. |
| 2½–3½ | Controlled scribbles, closed circles, marks named after the fact. |
| 3–4 | The tadpole person: head with legs, eyes first, then mouth and hair. |
| 4–6 | Bodies, clothes, floating objects, size by importance, the sun in the corner. |
| 6–9 | Baselines, repeated formulas, x-ray and fold-over pictures, real stories. |
| 9–12 | Realism, overlap, proportion, style copying, and the first “I can’t draw.” |

## Why the ages are a map, not a timetable

A range of a year or more on either side of every age above is ordinary. Children who draw a
lot move through the stages faster because the hand gets practice. Children with older siblings
copy ahead of themselves. A child who prefers building, climbing or talking may spend little
time with a pencil and arrive at each stage later, with nothing at all wrong.

What stays the same is the order. Children do not skip from scribbles to realism, and they
rarely go backward for long. A child who has drawn bodies for a year and briefly returns to
tadpoles is usually tired, rushing, or drawing for a much younger sibling.

## What is worth noticing

Instead of comparing your child with a chart or with a classmate, compare them with themselves.
Keep a few drawings with the date on the back and look at them three months apart. What you
are watching for is movement: a new detail that was never there before, a line that used to
wobble and now doesn’t, a figure that gained a neck. That progression is the developmental
content of a drawing, and it is visible to anyone who looks.

## When to talk to someone

Drawing on its own is almost never the reason to see a professional. Where it can be part of a
bigger picture is fine motor development. If a child of four or five shows no interest in any
kind of mark-making and also struggles with other hand tasks, scissors, buttons, stacking small
things, it is worth mentioning to your pediatrician at the next visit. The same goes for a
child who clearly loses skills they had. In both cases the drawing is a small piece of
evidence, and the person to weigh it is someone who can see the whole child.
