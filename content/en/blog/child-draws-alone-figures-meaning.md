faq:
  - q: Is it normal for a child to draw only themselves?
    a: Yes. Self-portraits and single figures dominate for years, and drawings with several people interacting arrive later than most parents expect. It is one of the most common things we are asked about.
  - q: Does drawing alone figures mean my child is lonely?
    a: Not on its own. Drawing one person is easier, faster and more satisfying than drawing five, and a child who is happily social all day will still hand you a single figure. Loneliness is something you would see in how your child lives, not in how many people fit on a page.
  - q: My child never draws me. Should I be hurt?
    a: No, and you are in good company. Families turn up in drawings when the drawing is about the family, which is a specific prompt rather than a default. Ask for a picture of everyone at dinner and you will usually get one.
  - q: Should I ask my child to add friends to the picture?
    a: Invite rather than instruct. “Who else was there?” works. Asking them to redo it teaches them the first version was wrong.
  - q: When is it worth mentioning to someone?
    a: When it comes with something else. If your child is also avoiding other children, seems flat or withdrawn, or something has changed at school, that is worth raising with your pediatrician or teacher whatever their drawings look like.

A lot of parents notice the same thing at once: every drawing has exactly one person in it.
No family, no friends, no scene. Just one figure, usually in the middle, sometimes very small.

It is one of the most over-interpreted patterns in children’s art, and one of the most
ordinary.

## Why one figure is the default

Drawing a person is hard work. Drawing five people who overlap, stand at different distances
and interact with each other is enormously harder, and it requires ideas about space that
arrive years after a child can draw a face.

So the single figure wins for practical reasons. It is also the thing children most want to
draw: themselves, a character they invented, or someone they admire. A child working out who
they are will draw themselves a great many times, and that is the point of the exercise rather
than a symptom of it.

## What changes with age

| Age | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| 2–4 | Single figures, floating, no ground line. Scenes with several people are rare. |
| 4–6 | Family members start appearing, often in a row, all the same size. |
| 6–9 | Interaction arrives: people facing each other, doing something together. |
| 9–12 | Portraits and invented characters often take over again, this time by choice. |

Notice that the social phase sits in the middle. A nine year old who has gone back to drawing
one detailed character has usually not regressed. They have found something they want to get
good at.

## What is actually worth noticing

None of these is a verdict, and none of them means anything by itself:

- The same figure drawn very small, in a corner, over and over.
- Your child being asked directly to draw the family, and steadily avoiding it.
- A stretch of solitary drawings that starts suddenly, alongside a change in how your child is
  at home or at school.
- Distress while drawing, rather than absorption.

The common thread there is that none of them is about the drawing on its own. Each one is a
combination of the page and the child, and the child is the part that matters.

## How to ask

- “Who is this? What are they up to?”
- “What happens next in this one?”
- “If someone came along, who would it be?”

Ask with genuine curiosity and leave room for a boring answer. “It’s just a guy” is a complete
and legitimate response, and a lot of drawings mean exactly that.

Avoid making the absence the subject. “Why is he all alone? Don’t you want to draw Mommy?”
turns a nice moment into a correction, and it teaches your child that drawings are checked.

## When to talk to someone

If your child is withdrawing from other children, if they seem flat or unhappy in ways that
last, or if something changed at school and has not changed back, talk to your pediatrician or
to a teacher who sees them every day. Those conversations are worth having on their own merits.
The drawings can come along, but they are not the reason and they are not the proof.
