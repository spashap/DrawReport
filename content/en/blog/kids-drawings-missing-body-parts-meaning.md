    a: Yes, and it stays normal for a surprisingly long time. Hands are among the hardest things on a body to draw, and children leave out what is hard long after they have noticed it is missing.
  - q: My 2 year old draws faces with no body. Is that a problem?
    a: That is a documented stage, often called the tadpole figure: a head with legs coming straight out of it, sometimes with arms. It is what almost every child draws first, usually somewhere between three and four, and it sorts itself out without any help.
  - q: What does it mean if my child draws people without eyes?

Your child hands you a drawing of the family and nobody has hands. Or eyes. Or, this week,
faces. It is one of the things parents notice first and worry about longest, and it is almost
always a story about drawing rather than a story about feelings.

## Why features go missing

**It is hard.** Hands are genuinely difficult. So are ears, noses and the place where an arm
meets a shoulder. Children skip what they cannot yet make look right, and they keep skipping
it for a while after they have noticed.

**They were in a hurry.** A child with a scene in their head is racing to get it down. Details
lose to plot every time.

**It is not the point.** Young children draw what matters to the picture. If the story is
about running, you get legs.

**It is the style.** Plenty of cartoons and comics leave out features on purpose, and older
children copy them deliberately.

## What is typical at each age

| Age | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| 2–3 | Scribbles that get named after the fact. No figures to be missing anything yet. |
| 3–4 | The tadpole figure: a head with legs, sometimes arms, no torso. Almost universal. |
| 4–6 | Bodies arrive. Faces get eyes and a mouth. Fingers are sticks, or absent. |
| 6–9 | Fuller figures, clothing, hair. Omissions shrink but survive in fast sketches. |
| 9–12 | Realism or deliberate stylization. Leaving things out becomes a choice. |

## About the specific ones

Parents usually arrive with one particular absence in mind, so here they are directly.

**No hands.** The most common of the lot, and the least interesting. Fingers are hard.

**No mouth.** Usually speed. A mouth is a small line that is easy to forget when the head is
already done.

**No eyes.** Often style, sometimes speed. Some children draw everyone in profile or with
closed eyes for months because they saw it somewhere.

**No nose or ears.** Standard at the younger end. These are small, they sit in the middle of
already-difficult areas, and nothing about the drawing needs them.

**A very small self, or no self at all.** This is the one worth a second look, though not for
the reason you might expect. Where a child puts themselves in a group drawing shifts with all
sorts of things, including who they were drawing for and how much room was left on the page.
It is worth noticing if it repeats. It is not worth interpreting on its own.

You will find articles that assign a meaning to each of these. A missing mouth means the child
feels unheard, a missing hand means helplessness. It reads well and there is no good evidence
for it. The honest version is that a five year old leaves off the hands because hands are hard.

## What is more worth your attention

- **Line quality.** Steady, or hesitant and redrawn?
- **Whether it is getting fuller over months.** Development shows up as a trend, not in one picture.
- **Whether your child is enjoying it.** Relaxed and chatty, or frustrated and erasing?
- **Whether the subjects change.** Variety across a week says more than any single sheet.

## How to talk about it

- “Tell me about this person. What are they doing?”
- “What was the trickiest bit to draw today?”
- “Are you going to add anything else to it?”

The one to avoid is the leading question. “Why doesn’t she have a mouth? Is it because you
feel like nobody listens?” gives your child an answer to agree with, and you will never know
what they would have said.

## When to mention it to someone

A missing feature is not a reason to call anyone. If your child seems withdrawn, if sleep or
appetite has changed, if school has become hard, or if they are distressed while drawing
rather than absorbed in it, that combination is worth raising with your pediatrician or a
teacher who sees them every day. Bring the drawings as context. They are a good conversation
starter and a poor piece of evidence.
