faq:
  - q: My child left me out of the family drawing. Does that mean something?
    a: Almost always it means you were not in the room, the page ran out, or you were going to be drawn last and they lost interest. Children draw who is in front of them and who is easiest to draw. If you want to know, ask “who else is in our family?” and watch how quickly you get added.
  - q: My child drew me the smallest. Should I be hurt?
    a: No. Size in a young child’s drawing follows the order things were drawn and how much page was left, far more than importance. Whoever came first got the most room. Dad may be tall because Dad is tall. The dog may be enormous because the dog is fun to draw.
  - q: My child drew a family that is not ours, with different people in it.
    a: Children draw families the way they draw houses, from a formula, and the formula often comes from a book or a show. A drawing of “a family” with a mom, a dad, two kids and a dog is a picture of the idea of a family, not a wish list. Ask who each person is before assuming.
  - q: The baby is bigger than everyone. Is that jealousy?
    a: More likely the baby was drawn first, or the baby is the most interesting thing in the house right now, or a round baby is easier to make large than a thin adult. A child who is struggling with a new sibling usually shows it at bedtime, not in the proportions of a drawing.
  - q: Should I ask my child to draw the family regularly to keep track?
    a: Once in a while, dated and kept, is a nice record of how their drawing develops. Regularly, as a check-up, will teach them that the drawing is a test, and children draw differently for tests. Ask for a picture of a good day instead and you will learn more.

Of all the drawings a child makes, the family portrait is the one parents study hardest. Why
am I smaller than the dog? Why is her brother missing? Why is she standing so far from the
rest of us? There is a whole folklore devoted to answering these questions, and most of it
should be set down gently.

## Why family drawings get over-read

The habit comes from the Kinetic Family Drawing, a projective test from 1970 that asked
children to draw their family doing something and then read the placement, size and omissions
as a map of the child’s feelings. Its ideas leaked into parenting culture and never left.

There is a grain of truth in one of them. Young children do tend to give more room to what
matters to them, so a favorite person, a new puppy or the child themselves can come out large.
But the same drawing is also shaped by which figure was drawn first, how much page was left,
who is tall in real life, and what is fun to draw. Importance is one voice among several, and
usually not the loudest.

## Who is missing

The most common reasons a family member is left out, roughly in order:

- **The page ran out**, or patience did. Family drawings are long projects for a five-year-old,
  and whoever was scheduled last often does not make it.
- **They were not there.** Children draw who is in the room. A parent at work at drawing time
  is easily left off.
- **The baby doesn’t count yet**, in the child’s system. Babies are not people who do things.
- **The sibling was annoying today.** Genuinely, and it will be reversed by Thursday.
- **They forgot themselves.** Children between four and six very often leave out the one
  person they cannot see, which is the artist.
- **The instruction was “draw a family,”** so they drew the idea of one, from a book.

An omission that repeats across many drawings over months is worth a curious question. A single
one is a page that ran out.

## Who is biggest, who came first

Watch a child draw a family and you will see the answer. The first figure gets the most room
and the most detail, because there is plenty of paper and plenty of energy. It is often the
child themselves, or the parent they spend most time with, or simply the figure they have
practiced the most. Everyone after that is squeezed in.

Height also follows reality more than the folklore allows. Children know Dad is tall. They
know the toddler is small. By six or seven they are trying to show it, imperfectly.

And some things are just satisfying to draw large. Dogs, babies, and anything round.

## Where people stand

Young children line people up in a row, holding hands or not, because a row is the formula for
“a group of people.” Spacing between figures reflects how much room was left and where the
hand happened to land. Grouping, when it appears around six or seven, usually reflects who does
things together in real life: the kids on one side, the parents on the other, the dog with
whoever feeds it.

Nothing reliable about closeness or attachment can be read from how far apart two figures
stand on a page. That claim has been tested, and it did not survive.

## What is actually visible

Set the folklore aside and a family drawing is still full of information, just of a different
kind.

**Where the detail went.** Who got eyelashes, pockets, a pattern on their shirt? Detail goes
where attention goes, and it also goes where the child’s drawing skill is most practiced.
Either way it tells you what they have been looking at.

**Whether people are different from each other.** A four-year-old draws the same figure five
times and labels them. A seven-year-old gives Mom her hair, Dad his glasses, the baby a
pacifier. That shift is a real developmental step: the child has started observing individual
people rather than drawing the idea of a person.

**What everyone is doing.** If the figures are doing things, the things are the story. Dad
cooking, the child on a bike, the cat asleep. That is a picture of what the household looks
like from about three feet off the ground.

**How the figures are built.** Bodies, necks, hands, feet that point somewhere. This is where
a family drawing shows you where your child is in the ordinary stages of drawing, and that is
the part that changes visibly from one year to the next.

## What is typical at each age

| Age | What usually shows up |
|---|---|
| 3–4 | Several tadpole figures, all alike, sometimes named after the fact. |
| 4–6 | A row of people with bodies, size by drawing order, frequent omissions, floating. |
| 6–9 | People stand on a baseline, doing things. Individuals become recognizable. |
| 9–12 | Real proportions, overlapping figures, self-conscious effort to get faces right. |

## How to ask

- “Who is this? And this one?”
- “What is everyone doing?”
- “Where are you in the picture?”
- “If the paper were bigger, who would you add?”

Avoid “Why didn’t you draw Daddy?” It turns a drawing into a test the child failed, and the
answer you get will be whatever ends the conversation fastest. “Who else is in our family?”
gets you the same information and usually a cheerful addition to the page.

## When it is worth a real conversation

Family drawings are a lovely way to talk with a child through a family change: a move, a new
baby, a separation, a loss. Draw together and let them tell you about it. The drawing is not
the diagnosis. It is the excuse for the conversation.

If a child consistently draws themselves absent, tiny or apart over weeks, and is also
withdrawn, sleeping badly or struggling at school, take the whole picture to your pediatrician,
and mention the drawings as one of the things you noticed. If a child tells you a figure in a
drawing hurts them, take that seriously, stay calm, and talk to a professional the same day.
The words matter far more than the picture, and they are rare. Most family drawings are
exactly what they look like: a row of people the child loves, in whatever order they came to
mind.
